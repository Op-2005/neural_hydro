\begin{abstract}
River basins form a physical network in which water flows downstream, so a basin's
discharge should depend on its upstream neighbors. Yet strong multi-basin LSTMs treat
every basin independently and match physics-based models, and when the river network is
added as graph structure it typically fails to help. We ask whether the network is
genuinely uninformative or is simply being supplied in the wrong form. To separate these,
we run a controlled ablation on stock NeuralHydrology \texttt{cudalstm}, byte-identical
across conditions, in which the only thing that changes is a single input carrying
network information---removing the architecture, training, and encoding confounds that
muddied prior attempts. On a 183-basin eastern-US sub-network of CAMELS-US, we find that
the network helps only as a \emph{dynamic} signal: adding a basin's fixed topological
position (graph depth, upstream count, upstream area) yields essentially no change in
skill, whether or not the model also has a basin-identity encoding, whereas feeding the
\emph{dynamic upstream discharge} arriving from upstream neighbors improves median test
NSE by $+0.038$ under an observed-flow oracle ($p=2.6\times10^{-17}$, three seeds). The
same network, opposite outcomes: the information lives in structure-as-flow, not
structure-as-label, which explains in one stroke why static graph approaches have failed.
The gain is real and deployable---it survives a shuffled-flow null control, holds in
log-NSE, and a two-stage model that \emph{predicts} upstream flow from forcings (no ground
truth at inference) recovers $+0.025$ NSE, about 55--72\% of the oracle ceiling and
positive across all three seeds. It is also mechanistic and specific to the real
network: the gain requires the true upstream connectivity---a degree-preserving random
rewiring of the graph removes most of it ($+0.034$ NSE for true vs.\ rewired edges,
$p=2\times10^{-14}$, three seeds)---and it rises with graph depth while vanishing at
headwaters, the signature of physical routing. The effect persists when the heuristic graph
is pruned to hydrography-realistic connectivity. We conclude that river-network structure
improves streamflow LSTMs when, and only when, it is delivered as a dynamic upstream-flow
signal tied to the real network, rather than as a static topological descriptor.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{streamflow prediction, LSTM, river-network topology, CAMELS}

\section{Methodology}

We ask whether river-network structure improves streamflow prediction, and in what form it must be
supplied. To answer this in isolation, every model we compare is the same trained LSTM, and the only
quantity that varies across conditions is one input channel.

\subsection{Setup and notation}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $V$ be a set of river basins, indexed by $i$ and $j$. For basin $i$ on day $t$ we observe five
daily meteorological forcings $\mathbf{m}_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^5$ (precipitation, shortwave radiation,
maximum and minimum temperature, and vapour pressure), a fixed vector of five static catchment
attributes $\mathbf{s}_i\in\mathbb{R}^5$ (mean elevation, drainage area, mean slope, mean
precipitation, and mean potential evapotranspiration), and observed discharge $q_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$
in mm\,d$^{-1}$, which is the prediction target. Following the multi-basin convention of
Kratzert et al.~\cite{kratzert2019}, each basin also carries a one-hot identity encoding
$\mathbf{e}_i\in\{0,1\}^{|V|}$ that lets a single model specialise to individual basins.

The task is next-day discharge: predict $q_i(t)$ from a length-$L$ window of inputs ending at $t$,
with $L=30$. Models are trained on 1990--1999, validated on 2000--2004, and evaluated on the
held-out 2005--2008 test period. All reported numbers are on the test period.

The river network is a directed acyclic graph $G=(V,E)$ whose edges point from upstream to
downstream. We write $\mathcal{P}(i)=\{\,j : (j\!\to\! i)\in E\,\}$ for the set of \emph{immediate
upstream parents} of basin $i$, and $A_i$ for its drainage area. Headwater basins have
$\mathcal{P}(i)=\varnothing$. Graph depth is defined recursively as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:depth}
\mathrm{depth}(i) =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } \mathcal{P}(i)=\varnothing,\\[2pt]
1 + \max_{j\in\mathcal{P}(i)} \mathrm{depth}(j) & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
so headwaters sit at depth $0$ and depth increases downstream. Depth indexes how much upstream flow
a basin receives, and we use it later to test whether any gain follows the routing structure.

\subsection{Base model}
\label{sec:model}

Every condition uses the same stock \texttt{cudalstm} model from the NeuralHydrology
library~\cite{kratzert2022joss}, the standard multi-basin LSTM for rainfall--runoff
modelling~\cite{kratzert2018,kratzert2019}. At day $t$, basin $i$'s inputs over the window
$t\!-\!L\!+\!1,\dots,t$ are concatenated into per-step vectors $\mathbf{x}_i(\cdot)$
(defined in \S\ref{sec:ablation}), passed through a single-layer LSTM with hidden size $H=64$, and
the final hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{H}$ is mapped to the prediction by a linear head:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:model}
\mathbf{h}_i(t) = \mathrm{LSTM}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_i(t\!-\!L\!+\!1),\dots,\mathbf{x}_i(t)\big),
\qquad
\hat{q}_i(t) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{h}_i(t) + b .
\end{equation}
We take the LSTM recurrence as standard~\cite{kratzert2018} and do not restate its gates. What
matters here is the interface: Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} consumes the input vector $\mathbf{x}_i$ that
our ablation modifies. Parameters are trained by minimising the mean squared error between
$\hat{q}_i(t)$ and $q_i(t)$ with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size $256$, $30$ epochs,
gradient-norm clipping at $1$, output dropout $0.4$, initial forget-gate bias $3$). Table~\ref{tab:config}
gives the full configuration, held identical across every condition.

\subsection{Study network and data}
\label{sec:network}

We use the CAMELS US catchment-attributes v2.0 dataset~\cite{addor2017}, which provides static
attributes for 671 contiguous-US catchments, together with the associated daily Maurer-product
forcings and observed streamflow~\cite{newman2015,newman2014data}. Ground-truth river-network
hydrography is not distributed with the attributes, so we infer directed edges from catchment
geometry. A directed edge $(j\!\to\!i)$ is placed when basin $i$ is plausibly downstream of $j$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:edge}
(j\!\to\!i)\in E \iff A_i \ge \rho\,A_j \;\wedge\; \mathrm{elev}(i) < \mathrm{elev}(j) \;\wedge\; d(i,j)\le D_{\max},
\end{equation}
with area ratio $\rho=1.5$, decreasing elevation, and a maximum inter-gauge distance
$D_{\max}=150$\,km. These edges are heuristic rather than surveyed hydrography. The graph controls
of \S\ref{sec:feature} and the results test whether the findings depend on their exact structure.
From the national graph we take the largest connected component, ``Component~0'': a $183$-basin
sub-network in the eastern US spanning six HUC regions, with $624$ inferred edges and depth up to
$4$. All experiments run on this network.

\subsection{The controlled ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

The network enters the model through the per-step input vector. Writing $u_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}$ for a
single scalar that carries structural information (defined in \S\ref{sec:feature}), each condition
uses
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:input}
\mathbf{x}_i(t) = \big[\;\mathbf{m}_i(t)\;\|\;\mathbf{s}_i\;\|\;\mathbf{e}_i\;\|\;u_i(t)\;\big],
\end{equation}
where $\|$ denotes concatenation. The baseline condition $\mathrm{L}$ omits the final coordinate.
Across every other condition the forcings, static attributes, and identity encoding are identical,
and \emph{only the last coordinate $u_i(t)$ changes.} Any difference in test skill between two
conditions is therefore attributable to $u_i(t)$ alone. This design removes the architecture,
training-budget, and encoding confounds that make results from bespoke graph models hard to
interpret: a measured difference reflects the structural signal, not the machinery around it.

\subsection{The structural signal}
\label{sec:feature}

The structural coordinate $u_i(t)$ aggregates the discharge of basin $i$'s immediate upstream
parents. We use the area-weighted mean of their discharge, lagged by $\tau=1$ day:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feature}
u_i(t) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}(i)} A_j\, q_j(t-\tau)}
      {\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}(i)} A_j}
  & \text{if } \mathcal{P}(i)\neq\varnothing,\\[14pt]
0 & \text{if } \mathcal{P}(i)=\varnothing.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Two properties of Eq.~\eqref{eq:feature} matter. First, it carries the units of the target: $A_j q_j$
has units km$^2\cdot$mm\,d$^{-1}$, and dividing by $\sum_j A_j$ returns mm\,d$^{-1}$, so $u_i$ lives
on the same scale as $q_i$. Taking the mean rather than the sum keeps a single large parent from
dominating. Second, and central to our claims, $u_i(t)$ is a \emph{fixed, directed, single-hop
precomputation}: its weights are drainage areas rather than learned parameters, its lag is fixed, it
reads only immediate graph predecessors, and it is computed once before training. It is not message
passing and performs no learned aggregation or iteration over the graph. The empirical question is
whether this minimal structural input helps where learned graph operators have not.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:feature} introduces no target leakage. It aggregates the discharge of \emph{upstream}
basins, so basin $i$'s own discharge never enters its input, and it is lagged, so no same-day
information is used.

The conditions of the ablation are instantiations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:feature} that differ only in the
discharge term $q_j$ or the parent set $\mathcal{P}(i)$, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:conditions}.
The \emph{oracle} uses observed upstream discharge and bounds the achievable gain.
The \emph{null} replaces $q_j(t-\tau)$ with the same series permuted in time, holding input scale and
capacity fixed while destroying temporal information. Two graph controls alter $\mathcal{P}(i)$: a
\emph{reversed} variant that draws parents from downstream neighbours (edges reversed), and a
\emph{random} variant that rewires edges while preserving each basin's in-degree. The \emph{realizable}
condition uses predicted rather than observed upstream discharge and is defined next.

\subsection{The deployable two-stage model}
\label{sec:realizable}

The oracle requires observed upstream discharge at inference, which a deployed forecaster does not
have. We therefore define a two-stage realizable model that uses only forcings at inference. In
stage one, the trained baseline $\mathrm{L}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} with the plain input) predicts
each basin's discharge from its own forcings and attributes over the full record, yielding
$\hat{q}_j(t)$ for all $j,t$. In stage two, we substitute these predictions into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:feature},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:realizable}
u_i^{\mathrm{real}}(t) =
\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}(i)} A_j\, \hat{q}_j(t-\tau)}
     {\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}(i)} A_j},
\end{equation}
and train a downstream model on inputs carrying $u_i^{\mathrm{real}}$. Because $\hat{q}_j$ depends
only on forcings, this model needs no observed discharge at inference. It recovers a fraction of the
oracle gain that we quantify in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Skill metrics}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate per basin on the test period with three complementary metrics. The primary metric is the
Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nse}
\mathrm{NSE}_i = 1 - \frac{\sum_t \big(q_i(t)-\hat{q}_i(t)\big)^2}{\sum_t \big(q_i(t)-\bar{q}_i\big)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{q}_i$ is the mean observed discharge. NSE takes values in $(-\infty,1]$, with $1$ a
perfect fit and $0$ the skill of predicting the mean. Because NSE is dominated by high flows, we also report a log-space
variant $\mathrm{logNSE}_i$, computed as Eq.~\eqref{eq:nse} on $\log(q+\epsilon)$ with
$\epsilon = 10^{-3}\cdot\max(\bar{q}_i,10^{-6})$ scaled to each basin's mean flow, and the
Kling--Gupta efficiency
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kge}
\mathrm{KGE}_i = 1 - \sqrt{(r-1)^2 + (\beta-1)^2 + (\gamma-1)^2},
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the correlation between $q_i$ and $\hat{q}_i$, $\beta=\bar{\hat q}_i/\bar q_i$ is the
bias ratio, and $\gamma=(\sigma_{\hat q_i}/\bar{\hat q}_i)/(\sigma_{q_i}/\bar q_i)$ the ratio of
coefficients of variation. KGE separates timing ($r$), bias ($\beta$), and variability ($\gamma$),
which we use to locate where a metric change originates. The comparison protocol built on these
metrics is described in \S\ref{sec:protocol}.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The value of a controlled ablation rests on the control actually holding. This section states the
protocol that makes each measured difference interpretable: an auditable single-variable design, a
pre-registered analysis plan, a paired multi-seed comparison, and a reproducible artifact trail. The
effect sizes we report are modest, so this apparatus is what separates a real signal from an
artifact, and we describe it in the detail a reader would need to reproduce or challenge our claims.

\subsection{A single-variable design, verified}
\label{sec:protocol-design}

Every condition is the model and training of Table~\ref{tab:config}, and the conditions differ only
in the input coordinate $u_i(t)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:input} (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). We do not take
this on trust: the run configurations are stored, and a direct configuration diff confirms that the
four headline conditions ($\mathrm{L}$, oracle, realizable, null) are identical in model, hidden
size, dropout, forget bias, optimiser, learning rate, batch size, epoch count, sequence length,
forcings, static attributes, identity encoding, split, and seed. The only field that differs is the
additional feature file supplying $u_i(t)$. This turns ``the difference-maker is our input'' from an
assertion into a checkable property of the configurations, and it is the specific control that a
bespoke graph model, with its own architecture and training loop, cannot offer.

\subsection{Pre-registration}
\label{sec:protocol-prereg}

Each experiment was pre-registered before its runs were observed. A registration fixes the
hypothesis, the success threshold, and the falsification criterion in advance, so that an outcome
cannot be reinterpreted after the fact. This discipline is load-bearing here, because it commits us
to reporting results that did not go our way. Two examples recur in the results. A null control was
registered to pass only if a time-shuffled upstream input produced negligible gain, which would have
undercut the whole study had it failed. A same-day-lag variant was registered as a stronger form of
the deployable model and then \emph{falsified}: it underperformed the one-day lag, and we report it
as such rather than dropping it. Falsified pre-registrations are reported, not redesigned.

\subsection{Comparison protocol}
\label{sec:protocol-compare}

Our unit of comparison is the paired, per-basin change in skill against the shared baseline. For a
condition $c$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:delta}
\Delta_i^{(c)} = \mathrm{NSE}_i(c) - \mathrm{NSE}_i(\mathrm{L}).
\end{equation}
Pairing per basin removes basin-level difficulty and isolates the effect of $u_i(t)$: a basin that is
hard for $\mathrm{L}$ is equally hard for $c$, and only the change matters. We summarise a condition
by the cross-basin median of $\Delta_i^{(c)}$ and test it against zero with a one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test, a paired non-parametric test appropriate for the skewed, non-Gaussian distribution
of per-basin skill. Every condition is trained under three seeds ($11,13,17$). We report the
cross-seed mean and standard deviation of the median and pool basins across seeds for the
significance tests. A structural claim is credited only when it holds across seeds, not in one.

\subsection{Reproducibility and compute}
\label{sec:protocol-repro}

The study runs entirely on a stock model with published configurations, so its artifacts are
complete and its cost is modest: each condition is a single \texttt{cudalstm} trained for $30$
epochs on $183$ basins, and the full study is a few dozen such runs across conditions and seeds. We
retain, for every run, the exact configuration and the per-basin test predictions and metrics, so
that every number in this paper can be recomputed from stored outputs without retraining. The
structural inputs are deterministic functions of the released discharge and the inferred graph, and
the graph follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge}. Both are regenerable from the public dataset. Code and
configurations are released.
